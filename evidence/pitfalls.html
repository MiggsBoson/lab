1The prosecutor’s fallacy

“The chance of a match if the suspect is innocent is 1 in a million. So the chance the suspect is innocent is 1 in a million.”

This swaps P(match | innocent) for P(innocent | match), the transposed conditional. In a city of 5 million, a 1-in-a-million profile would be shared by about 5 other people. If the match were the only evidence, the suspect would be one of about 6 people who match, so the chance of being the source would be about 1 in 6, not 999,999 in a million. Thompson and Schumann (1987) gave the error its name.

Fix: report the likelihood ratio, or how many other people would be expected to match, and leave the prior to the court.

Try it: worked example, step 4 · seeing it with counts · Cases: R v Deen, Sally Clark

2The defence fallacy

“About 5 other people in the city would match. So the suspect has only a 1 in 6 chance of being the source, and the DNA is worthless.”

The DNA has taken the suspect from 1 in 5 million to 1 in 6. That is a likelihood ratio of a million, 6 bans. The remaining doubt has to be settled by the rest of the evidence, such as who had access to the scene, not by discarding this item. Thompson and Schumann named this one too.

A famous variant came up in the O. J. Simpson trial (1995). The defence argued that very few men who beat their wives go on to murder them. I. J. Good pointed out in Nature that the relevant question is different: of battered women who are murdered, how many were killed by their batterer? That fraction is far larger, because the wife had in fact been murdered.

Fix: combine the evidence with everything else, rather than judging it alone against the whole population.

See: defence fallacy in the glossary · the prior

3Base-rate neglect

“The drug dog is right 90% of the time, so a car it alerts on is 90% likely to be carrying drugs.”

Suppose (illustrative numbers) 1 car in 100 carries drugs, the dog alerts on 90% of those, and wrongly on 10% of the rest. Out of 1,000 cars, 10 carry drugs and the dog alerts on 9 of them. Of the 990 clean cars, it alerts on 99. So of the 108 alerts, only 9 are right: about 8%, not 90%. The rarity of what you are looking for, the base rate, dominates.

Fix: turn percentages into counts out of a round number (natural frequencies). The answer usually becomes obvious.

Try it: seeing it with counts

4The independence trap

“The chance of one cot death in a family like this is 1 in 8,543, so the chance of two is 1 in 8,543², about 1 in 73 million.”

Multiplying probabilities is only valid for independent events. A family that has lost one baby to sudden infant death may share genes and circumstances that make a second death more likely, so P(second | first) is much bigger than P(first). The product rule says to multiply by P(second | first). Squaring badly overstated how rare two natural deaths were. The same mistake inflated the figures in People v Collins, where features such as a beard and a moustache, which tend to go together, were multiplied as if they had nothing to do with each other.

Fix: multiply only conditional probabilities, P(A) × P(B | A), and say where each comes from.

Try it: worked example, step 6 · competing explanations · Cases: Sally Clark, Collins

5Numerical conversion

“A 1-in-a-million profile means you would have to test a million people to find another match.”

Among a million unrelated people about one match is expected, and the chance that at least one of them matches is 1 − (1 − 1/1,000,000)1,000,000, about 63%. Koehler (1993) described this numerical conversion error. The same sum appears in Collins: even taking the prosecutor’s 1 in 12 million at face value, the court’s own appendix worked out that, if one couple in the area fitted the description, the chance of at least one more was about 40%.

Fix: work out how many matches you would expect in the relevant population. The DNA module’s “other matching people expected” tile does exactly this.

Try it: DNA profile, then change the population

6Forgetting error rates

“The profile has a match probability of 1 in a billion, so the likelihood ratio is a billion.”

A reported match can also be a false positive: contamination, a mislabelled tube, a misread result. If that happens once in every thousand cases, the probability of a reported match from someone who isn’t the source is about 1 in a billion plus 1 in a thousand. The larger of the two wins, so the LR is about 1,000, not a billion (Thompson, Taroni and Aitken 2003).

LR ≈ 1 ÷ (match probability + false-positive probability)

Fix: know your method’s error rate, measured by blind proficiency tests, and include it. A one-in-a-billion figure only means something when errors are rarer still.

7Double counting

“The fragments’ refractive index matches the window (LR 20), and they are a 1-in-100 type of glass (×100), so together that’s 2,000.”

If the measurement LR already reflects how rare that refractive index is, the 1-in-100 type is the same information counted twice. Likewise, evidence used to narrow the list of suspects (the prior) shouldn’t be counted again as evidence.

Fix: let each piece of information in once. Put related items into one model, as the transfer module does with p and p꜀.

8“Consistent with”

“The fibres on the victim are consistent with the suspect’s jumper.”

“Consistent with” only says that P(E | Hp) isn’t tiny. Unless you also know how probable the fibres would be from some other source, P(E | Hd), the statement has no weight. Blue cotton fibres are consistent with millions of jumpers.

Fix: always ask “and how probable is that if the other account is true?”. Then give the ratio.

See: the likelihood ratio

9Individualisation

“This print was made by the suspect’s finger, to the exclusion of every other finger in the world.”

Nobody has compared a mark against every finger in the world. Even if every finger is unique, a smudged partial mark can look like the wrong one, and examiners make mistakes. That is the uniqueness fallacy. US reviews by the National Academy of Sciences (2009) and the President’s Council of Advisors on Science and Technology (PCAST, 2016) criticised claims of individualisation and of zero error rates in fingerprints, firearms and other comparison methods.

Fix: report the strength of support, backed by error rates measured in properly designed tests.

Case: Brandon Mayfield

10Context and bias

“The detective mentioned he had confessed, but that didn’t affect my comparison.”

Contextual bias is not dishonesty; it happens to careful experts. In a study by Dror, Charlton and Péron (2006), experienced fingerprint examiners were shown prints they themselves had matched years earlier, but told the prints came from a notorious misidentification. Most of them reached a different conclusion the second time.

Fix: linear sequential unmasking: study and record the crime-scene mark before seeing the suspect’s reference, and hear other case information only when it is needed. Check conclusions by blind verification.

Case: Brandon Mayfield

11Data chosen after the fact

“She was on duty for so many incidents that the chance of coincidence is 1 in 342 million.”

If the incidents are gathered after suspicion has fallen on someone, the data are shaped by the hypothesis they are meant to test. That is the Texas sharpshooter: draw the target around the bullet holes, then admire the grouping. Across thousands of nurses and wards, some nurse somewhere will be on shift for an unusual cluster by chance. And a tiny p value is P(data | chance), not P(chance | data).

Fix: define the events and how they will be collected before looking. Ask how often a cluster like this would turn up for someone, not just for this person.

Case: Lucia de Berk · p-values and posteriors

§Real cases

Short summaries of cases in which these errors mattered, oldest first. Each one links back to the pitfalls it shows.

California, USA · robbery 1964 · Supreme Court of California 1968

People v Collins

Witnesses to a robbery in Los Angeles described a woman with a blonde ponytail and a man with a beard and moustache driving off in a yellow car. At trial a mathematics instructor multiplied six probabilities for these features, figures the prosecutor had suggested without any data, to get 1 in 12 million. The California Supreme Court reversed the conviction: the figures had no basis, the features were not independent, and even on their own terms they did not show that the couple on trial was the only one that fitted.

England · Court of Appeal, December 1993

R v Deen

A rape conviction resting largely on DNA was quashed because the match probability had been presented to the jury as if it were the probability that someone other than the defendant was the source. It was one of the first cases where the Court of Appeal explained the prosecutor’s fallacy in terms.

Prosecutor’s fallacy
England · Court of Appeal, 1996 and 1997

R v Adams

The only evidence against Denis Adams was a DNA match, quoted at 1 in 200 million. The victim did not pick him out at an identity parade, and he had an alibi. The defence called a statistician who took the jury through Bayes’ theorem so they could combine the DNA with that other evidence. The Court of Appeal ordered a retrial, and in its judgments said this approach should not be used with juries: weighing the non-scientific evidence is the jury’s own task. In the same year, in R v Doheny and Adams, the court gave guidance on presenting DNA figures, such as how many people in the country might share the profile.

The priorDefence fallacy
England · convicted 1999 · conviction quashed 2003

Sally Clark

Sally Clark was convicted of murdering her two baby sons. A paediatrician told the jury that the chance of two cot deaths in a family like hers was 1 in 73 million, by squaring 1 in 8,543. In 2001 the Royal Statistical Society publicly criticised the figure: the deaths were not independent, and the chance of two natural deaths is not the chance of innocence, since double murder is also extremely rare. The conviction was quashed in 2003, after medical evidence that had not been disclosed came to light.

Netherlands · convicted 2003 · acquitted 2010

Lucia de Berk

A paediatric nurse was convicted of murdering patients, supported by a statistical argument that so many incidents on her shifts had a 1 in 342 million chance of being coincidence. The incidents had been collected after suspicion fell on her, and statisticians, Richard Gill among them, showed that the calculation was flawed in several ways. The case was reopened in 2008, and she was acquitted in 2010.

Data chosen afterwardsp-values
USA and Spain · 2004

Brandon Mayfield

After the Madrid train bombings, FBI examiners identified a fingerprint on a bag of detonators as belonging to Brandon Mayfield, an Oregon lawyer, and other examiners confirmed it. The Spanish National Police then matched the print to another man. The FBI withdrew the identification and later apologised. A US Justice Department review found that the computer search had turned up an unusually similar print, and that the examiners who checked the identification already knew what the first examiner had concluded.

IndividualisationContext and bias
England · Court of Appeal, 2010

R v T

A footwear examiner worked out a likelihood ratio from data on how common the shoe’s pattern and size were, combined with judgements about wear, but reported only the words “moderate support” without saying how they were reached. The Court of Appeal criticised the lack of transparency, and said such formulas should not be used for footwear without a firm statistical basis. Forensic statisticians replied (Berger and colleagues 2011) that the likelihood ratio is the logic of weighing evidence whether or not there are good data. The argument over how far numbers can and should be used continues.

✓Spot the fallacy

Name the error in each statement, then open it to check.

“The DNA profile occurs in 1 in 10 million people, so there is only a 1 in 10 million chance he is innocent.”

Prosecutor’s fallacy. 1 in 10 million is P(match | not the source). How likely the suspect is to be the source also depends on how many other people could have left the stain, and on the other evidence.

“About 50 people in the country share this blood type, so the blood evidence is irrelevant.”

Defence fallacy. Going from tens of millions of possible sources to 50 is a huge likelihood ratio. It has to be combined with the other evidence, not thrown away.

“Each of three witness descriptions fits 1 person in 10, so an innocent person would fit all three with probability 1 in 1,000.”

Independence trap. Descriptions such as height and build go together, so P(all three) is bigger than 1/10 × 1/10 × 1/10.

“The lab’s match probability is 1 in a billion, so the chance of a wrong match is 1 in a billion.”

Forgetting error rates. A mix-up or contamination is far more likely than 1 in a billion, and caps the likelihood ratio.

“The injuries are consistent with a fall.”

“Consistent with”. Are they also consistent with an assault? Without the probability under the alternative there is no likelihood ratio.

“The glass matched on refractive index, LR 20. It’s also a rare type, 1 in 100, so the combined LR is 2,000.”

Double counting, if the refractive index LR already accounts for how rare the glass is. Count each piece of information once.
