1Two questions that sound alike

Glass on a suspect’s jacket matches a broken window. There are two questions you could ask about it.

  • The scientist’s question. How probable are these findings if the glass came from that window? And how probable if it came from somewhere else?
  • The court’s question. Given these findings, how probable is it that the glass came from that window?

They sound alike, but they differ the way “how probable is it that a dog has four legs?” differs from “how probable is it that a four-legged animal is a dog?”. The first is nearly 1. The second is small, because cats, horses and tables have four legs too.

Bayes’ theorem is the rule that gets you from the first kind of question to the second. It also says exactly what else you need to get there: how probable things were before this evidence came along. The rest of this page builds that up one piece at a time.

2Probability, conditioning and odds

A probability is a number from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain) that says how strongly to expect something, given what you know. Write it P(A).

A conditional probability, P(A | B), is the probability of A once you know B is true. Read the bar as “given”. To work it out, keep only the cases where B happened, and ask what fraction of those also have A:

P(A | B) = P(A and B) ÷ P(B)

The order matters. P(four legs | dog) ≈ 1, while P(dog | four legs) is small. Swapping the two is called the transposed conditional, and in a courtroom it is the prosecutor’s fallacy.

Two events are independent if knowing one happened doesn’t change the probability of the other. Only then may you multiply: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). In general you need P(A) × P(B | A).

Odds are another way to write a probability: the chance something is true divided by the chance it is false. They look odd at first, but they turn Bayes’ theorem into a single multiplication.

3Bayes’ theorem

Take a hypothesis H and some evidence E. Bayes’ theorem says:

P(H | E) = P(E | H) × P(H) ÷ P(E) posterior = likelihood × prior ÷ (total probability of the evidence)

The prior P(H) is how probable H was before the evidence. The likelihood P(E | H) is how probable the evidence is if H is true. The posterior P(H | E) is how probable H is afterwards.

The awkward part is P(E), the overall probability of the evidence. Forensic work avoids it by comparing two propositions: Hp, the prosecution’s, and Hd, the defence’s. Write Bayes’ theorem for each and divide one by the other. P(E) is on both lines, so it cancels:

P(Hp | E) P(E | Hp) P(Hp) ───────── = ───────── × ───── P(Hd | E) P(E | Hd) P(Hd) posterior odds = likelihood ratio × prior odds

This one line is the whole framework. The middle term, the likelihood ratio, is about the evidence, and the scientist can work it out. The last term, the prior odds, is about everything else in the case, and that is for the court. Neither can do the other’s job.

The posterior after one item becomes the prior for the next. Updating item by item gives the same answer as updating on everything at once, so the order in which evidence arrives makes no difference.

4Seeing it with counts

People find Bayes’ theorem much easier with counts than with percentages (natural frequencies; Gigerenzer and Hoffrage 1995). So picture everyone who could have left a bloodstain. One of them did. Everyone else has a small chance of matching it by accident. Now count the people who match.

Try this: keep the match probability at 1 in 100,000 and slide the population from 100 up to 10 million. Nothing about the blood changes, yet the chance that the matching suspect is the source falls from almost certain to almost nothing. That is the prior at work.

5The likelihood ratio: the scientist’s number

LR = P(E | Hp) ÷ P(E | Hd)

The likelihood ratio says how many times more probable the findings are if Hp is true than if Hd is true.

  • LR above 1 supports Hp. Below 1 supports Hd. Exactly 1 means the evidence can’t tell them apart: it has no probative value, however striking it looks.
  • Both halves matter. Suppose the stain is a blood type that about half of people share, and so does the suspect. The findings are certain if the suspect is the source (P = 1), and have a probability of 0.5 if someone else is. The LR is 2: weak support. The blood is entirely “consistent with” him, and yet it tells you almost nothing. “Consistent with” is only half the story.
  • It describes the evidence, not the propositions. Correct: “the findings are 1,000 times more probable if the suspect is the source.” Wrong: “the suspect is 1,000 times more likely to be the source.” The second is only true if the prior odds were exactly 1.

In general statistics the same ratio is called a Bayes factor. When a hypothesis has unknown parameters, each side becomes a probability averaged over them (a marginal likelihood).

In the workbench every module ends in a likelihood ratio card: measurement match, transfer, DNA.

6Choosing the propositions

A likelihood ratio is only as meaningful as the pair of propositions it compares. Hp and Hd should not both be true, they should matter to the case, and where possible they should be set before the results are known. “Someone else” needs spelling out. An unrelated person from the town and the suspect’s brother give very different LRs for the same DNA match, because brothers share far more DNA.

Propositions can also be pitched at different levels. This is the hierarchy of propositions (Cook and colleagues 1998, and the ENFSI guideline):

Higher levels are closer to what the court cares about, but need more knowledge about how material moves and lasts. The same DNA can be strong at sub-source level and weak at activity level, for example if the suspect had innocently visited the shop the week before.

7Weight of evidence: bans and words

Likelihood ratios span huge ranges, from about 2 to a billion, so it helps to take logarithms. The weight of evidence is log₁₀ LR, measured in bans. Alan Turing and I. J. Good used this unit at Bletchley Park to weigh evidence about Enigma settings.

W = log₁₀ LR prior log-odds + W₁ + W₂ + … = posterior log-odds

One ban multiplies the odds by 10. Independent items multiply their LRs, so their weights simply add, like weights on a scale. An LR of 2 weighs 0.3 bans; a one-in-a-million DNA match weighs 6.

Reports also put the LR into words. This tool uses the verbal scale from the ENFSI guideline (2015):

8The prior: whose job is it?

The prior sums up everything known apart from this evidence: who had the chance, what witnesses said, alibis, other forensic results. Setting it is the job of the judge or jury. The scientist reports the LR and leaves the prior alone.

To show how an LR plays out, a neutral starting point is often used: the offender is one of N people, all equally likely, so the prior odds are 1 to N − 1. The workbench’s case summary works that way. Real cases have more to go on.

The same evidence can lead to very different conclusions. An LR of a million takes a prior of 1 in 100 to 99.99%, but a prior of 1 in 5 million only to about 17%. Checking how much a conclusion depends on its inputs is a sensitivity analysis. The last step of the worked example does one.

In court. Judges in England have been wary of asking juries to do Bayesian arithmetic themselves (R v Adams). Experts usually give the LR, or a count such as “about 5 other people in the city would be expected to match”, and leave the combining to the jury.

9Combining evidence

Items of evidence can be combined by multiplying their LRs, or adding their weights, if they are conditionally independent: once you know whether Hp or Hd is true, learning one item tells you nothing more about the other.

  • Usually fine: DNA results at different loci, roughly, with θ allowing for shared ancestry. Glass on a jacket and an unrelated shoe mark.
  • Not fine: the number of glass fragments and their type. Both depend on whether glass was transferred, so they belong in one transfer model (worked example, step 3). In People v Collins, a beard and a moustache were treated as if they had nothing to do with each other.
  • Never: the same information twice. That is double counting, and it includes evidence already used to set the prior.

When several items depend on each other, a Bayesian network keeps track of the dependencies and applies Bayes’ theorem across all of them.

10Measurements: densities, not probabilities

A glass fragment’s refractive index is 1.5226. The probability of exactly 1.5226 is zero under any smooth model, so what can P(E | Hp) mean? It means how densely each source produces values near 1.5226: the heights of the two curves at that point, their probability densities.

LR = f(x | Hp) ÷ f(x | Hd) P(x within ±dx | H) ≈ f(x | H) × 2dx, and the 2dx cancels in the ratio

This approach goes back to Lindley (1977) and Evett. Two consequences:

  • Rarity matters as much as closeness. For normal curves, LR = (σ/s)·exp((z_σ² − z_s²)/2). A close match (small z_s) to a value that is rare in the population (large z_σ) gives a big LR. A close match to a common value does not.
  • Units don’t matter. Rescale the measurement and both densities pick up the same stretch factor, the Jacobian, which cancels. The LR is the same in any units.

See both in step 2 of the worked example. The measurement module’s interval method shows the window version and how it approaches the density result as the window shrinks.

11Estimating a quantity: time since death

Bayes’ theorem also works for a quantity that can take any value, like the time t since death. Put a prior over t, multiply by the likelihood of the measurement at each t, and rescale so the total is 1:

p(t | reading) ∝ p(reading | t) × p(t)

The result is a whole curve of belief. A 95% credible interval is a range holding 95% of it.

Suppose a rectal thermometer reads T with an error of about ±σ. The likelihood is a bell curve around the cooling curve: high at times when the body would have been near T. That leaves the prior. Two reasonable-sounding choices:

  • Flat in time: before the reading, every hour in the window is equally likely.
  • Flat in temperature: every true body temperature is equally likely. Written in terms of time, this prior is the cooling rate |dT/dt|, the Jacobian of the change of variable. It is what the workbench computes when it pushes the thermometer’s error bar through the cooling curve.

Room 12 °C, body 75 kg, rectal model as in the workbench.

Flat in timeFlat in temperature (the workbench’s orange curve)

Just after death, on the post-mortem plateau, the curve is flat and |dT/dt| is close to 0. The flat-in-temperature prior gives the first minutes very little weight; the flat-in-time prior doesn’t. The reading fits all those early times about equally well, so the prior decides how much belief they get. In this simplified model the plateau lasts about an hour, so that is where the disagreement sits; in real bodies the plateau often lasts longer. Later on, where the cooling curve is roughly straight across the likely range, the two priors agree and the data decide.

The lesson: “I have no idea” isn’t one prior. It depends on which variable you are ignorant about, and the Jacobian is how a prior that is flat in one variable looks in another. A likelihood ratio, by contrast, doesn’t care which variable you use (section 10).

Try 31 °C, where the priors agree, and a larger thermometer error at 37 °C, where they disagree more. Then open the time-since-death module: its orange curve is the orange one here.

12Bayesian and frequentist statistics

There are two common readings of probability. Frequentist: the long-run frequency of an outcome over repeated trials. Bayesian: a degree of belief, given the information available. Forensic evaluation needs the second, because “did this glass come from that window?” happened once. But the numbers that go into it come from frequencies: surveys of glass, DNA databases, transfer experiments.

  • A p value is P(data at least this extreme | nothing going on). It is not P(nothing going on | data). Reading it that way is the prosecutor’s fallacy again, and a tiny p-value applied to data chosen after the fact helped convict Lucia de Berk.
  • A confidence interval comes from a method that would contain the true value 95% of the time over many repeats. A credible interval contains it with 95% probability, given this data and a prior. With lots of data and a vague prior they are often close, but they answer different questions.
  • Common ground: both schools are built on the likelihood, P(data | hypothesis). Their disagreement is over what to do with it.

13If you know statistical physics

Aside

Write the posterior as a Boltzmann distribution, p(θ | E) = e−S(θ) / Z, with “action” S(θ) = −ln P(E | θ) − ln p(θ). The normalising constant Z = ∫ e−S dθ is the marginal likelihood.

  • A Bayes factor between hypotheses with free parameters is a ratio of partition functions, Zp/Zd. Estimating it is the same problem as a free-energy difference, and thermodynamic integration (path sampling; Gelman and Meng 1998) is a standard way to do it.
  • Markov chain Monte Carlo, including Hybrid (Hamiltonian) Monte Carlo from lattice field theory, samples a posterior the way it samples field configurations. Posterior means are expectation values.
  • Changing variables brings a Jacobian into the measure. Drop it and you have quietly changed the prior, as in section 11. In supersymmetric models the fermion determinant plays this part: it is the Jacobian of the Nicolai map from fields to noise.

✓Check yourself

An expert reports an LR of 1,000. Is the suspect 1,000 times more likely to be the source than not?

No. The evidence is 1,000 times more probable if the suspect is the source. The odds that the suspect is the source are 1,000 × the prior odds. Starting from 1 in 100,000, that comes to about 1 in 100.

The prior odds are 1 to 999 and the LR is 1,000. What is the probability afterwards?

Posterior odds = 1,000 × 1/999 ≈ 1, so about 50%. A “strong” LR has only brought a long shot up to a coin toss.

Two independent items have LRs of 20 and 50. What do they weigh together?

20 × 50 = 1,000, which is 3 bans: 1.3 + 1.7. Multiplying LRs is adding weights.

An LR is 0.05. Which proposition does it support, and how strongly?

Hd, because it is below 1. 1/0.05 = 20, so it is moderate support for Hd, a weight of −1.3 bans.

A report says fibres on a victim are “consistent with” the suspect’s jumper. What is missing?

How probable it would be to find such fibres if they came from somewhere else. Without P(E | Hd) there is no likelihood ratio, so the statement has no weight. Common fibres are consistent with thousands of jumpers.

Why doesn’t the scientist need P(E), the overall probability of the evidence?

In the odds form of Bayes’ theorem it appears on the top and bottom and cancels. Only P(E | Hp) and P(E | Hd) are needed.

A reading of 37.0 °C: why do the two priors in section 11 disagree?

37.0 °C is on the plateau, where the cooling curve is almost flat. The flat-in-temperature prior is proportional to |dT/dt|, which is nearly 0 there, so it pushes belief away from the first minutes. The flat-in-time prior doesn’t. The reading fits every early time about equally, so the prior decides. At 31 °C, on the straight part of the curve, they agree.
