Bad luck or a big player?

When two prices that normally move together drift apart, someone usually asks whether a large trader was pushing them. Investigators answer with a likelihood ratio: how many times more probable the price record is if a big player was there. Computing that number has a trap in it, and the fix comes from quantum field theory.

Flagged means a likelihood ratio of 100 or more, which forensic guidelines call “moderately strong support”. Results come from 4,000 simulated markets calibrated to real oil prices.

Real prices

Two oils, one gap

Brent is crude oil priced in the North Sea. WTI is crude priced at Cushing, Oklahoma. They are nearly the same product, so their prices track each other and the gap between them usually sits at a few dollars a barrel. Stretch the gap and it drifts back, like a rubber band.

What a big player does

One trader can change the shape of the future

Add a single large trader whose position has a limit. A stabiliser bets that the gap will close, so it trades against every move. An amplifier trades with the move: a fund forced to unwind a losing bet, a wave of stop-loss orders, or a momentum strategy.

When big players amplified real moves

1998

LTCM and the Royal Dutch/Shell gap

Long-Term Capital Management held a large bet that the price gap between Royal Dutch and Shell shares, two listings of one business, would close. In 1998 the gap widened while the fund was losing money elsewhere, and a group of banks recapitalised it that September.

MacKenzie, ch. 8 · RBA RDP 2003-06
6 May 2010

The Flash Crash

A fund's algorithm began selling 75,000 E-mini S&P 500 futures, about $4.1 billion, at 9% of market volume without regard to price or time. Regulators found that in stressed markets such selling can trigger extreme price moves.

SEC and CFTC staff report
March 2021

Archegos

After the family office Archegos failed to meet margin calls, banks sold more than $20 billion of its shares in large blocks, pushing prices down further. Credit Suisse alone lost about $5.5 billion.

Credit Suisse special committee report
20 April 2020

WTI below zero

The May WTI futures contract settled at −$37.63 the day before it expired. The CFTC's interim staff report set out the conditions but did not identify a root cause, and one commissioner called it incomplete. Bad luck or big players remained an open question.

CFTC release · Berkovitz statement

Weighing the evidence

Scoring a price record like a forensic scientist

Suppose all the investigator has is the daily gap. They compare two explanations. Bad luck: ordinary news moved the gap. Big player: an amplifier pushing up to $0.18 a day was active. The likelihood ratio says how many times more probable the record is under one explanation than the other, and the ENFSI guideline for forensic reporting turns it into words.

There are two ways to score each day. Surprise only works out what news the day's move needed and asks how unusual that news is. With the stretch correction also accounts for how the big player would have stretched that news into a bigger or smaller move.

Each case is the simulated record closest to the typical result for its group, not a hand-picked extreme. Likelihood ratios read as “times more probable with a big player”; 1/14 means 14 times more probable with bad luck.

With the correction, innocent markets drift towards “support for bad luck” as evidence piles up, which is what should happen. Without it, the same markets drift the other way. The error grows with every extra day, so more data makes the conclusion more confidently wrong.

Detection is also genuinely hard. Using prices alone, the corrected score flags about a third of real big players after a full year. Real investigations add order books, account records and communications.

The idea

Every model stretches its news

A world map has the same problem. On a Mercator map Greenland looks about as big as Africa, although Africa is roughly fourteen times larger. To compare real areas you multiply by how much the map stretches each region. Mathematicians call that stretch factor the Jacobian.

A price model is a map from news to price paths. Where the amplifier is active, a little news becomes a large move. Scoring a path by its news alone forgets that stretching, so it treats the stretched regions as more likely than they are.

Physicists meet the same factor when they compute with fermions, where it is called the fermion determinant. That gave a way to check the method: answer one question three ways.

Chance the gap ends more than $3 from normal after 60 daysNo big playerAmplifier

Replaying the future generates the news first and lets the market respond, so the stretch is handled automatically. Scoring whole paths directly, which is how physicists sample with Hybrid Monte Carlo, matches the replay only when the determinant is included.

What this leaves out

Read the result with these limits in mind

  • Bell-curve news. The 20 April 2020 move would be essentially impossible in this model.
  • A known suspect. The investigator compares one specific type of big player against none. Real cases weigh many possibilities.
  • Prices only. Surveillance teams use orders, accounts and communications, which carry far more evidence than prices do.
  • A likelihood ratio is not a verdict. It says how much the evidence should shift the odds. Turning it into a probability of guilt needs prior odds and the other evidence, and confusing the two is the prosecutor's fallacy.
  • One calibration. The rubber band is fitted to 2021–2025 daily data with one simulation step per trading day.
